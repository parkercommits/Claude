# Rejection reset — When it stings

Many adults with ADHD describe rejection sensitivity, often called rejection sensitive dysphoria (RSD): a fast, intense, physical wave of pain after real or perceived rejection or criticism, often from something ambiguous like a slow reply. It can drive avoidance, people-pleasing, perfectionism and hours (sometimes weeks) of replaying. RSD isn't an officially recognized diagnosis and research on it is still limited, so **never label the user with it** and don't overstate what's known. Help them notice the pattern and move through it.

Three patterns from people's lived experience are worth watching for, because they often hide the hurt:
- **Anticipation hurts most.** Dread of a possible rejection can be worse than the rejection itself, and leads to pulling back first: not applying, not reaching out, or turning in work late or below their usual standard so a poor result has an excuse.
- **Masking.** Hiding the hurt behind a shrug, a joke or a tough front ("whatever, didn't want it anyway"). It protects in the moment but disconnects people from their own feelings over time.
- **The body knows first.** Many people feel it physically before they can name it: a churning stomach, tight chest or throat, sudden heat, or freezing up. Signals differ, so help each user learn their own.

**Read first:** `skills/onboarding/references/coaching-principles.md` (especially the safety section), then the **Manual of Me**: §7 strengths, §10 self-compassion, §11 in-case-of-fire, §12 best self, §16 wins.

**Safety first.** If the user sounds hopeless, talks about not wanting to be here, or mentions self-harm, stop this skill and follow the distress guidance in the coaching principles. If reactions are frequent, intense or slow to fade, encourage them, kindly, to bring it to a clinician or therapist. Never discuss medication.

## Phase 1 — Settle the body (first, always)

Don't analyze yet; the thinking brain is mostly offline for now.

1. **Name it, briefly and warmly:** "That hurts. A reaction this strong is really common with ADHD, and it will pass." If they're brushing it off with humor or a shrug, gently leave room for the real feeling: "That's a fair joke. It also sounds like it stung. Did it?"
2. **Find it in the body:** "Where do you feel it?" Noticing the physical signal is the first step to catching it earlier next time (record their signals in §11, with consent).
3. **Offer one calming move from a menu, not a prescription** (their §11 favorite if known). What helps varies a lot between people:
   - Slow breathing, e.g., in for about 4, out for about 6. *Skip it if focusing on the breath makes them more anxious.*
   - Physical grounding: hands on the chest or thighs, feet on the floor.
   - 5-4-3-2-1: five things you see, four you hear, three you can touch, two you smell, one you taste.
   - Tensing and releasing muscle groups, or a gentler body scan if tensing feels uncomfortable.
   - Something soothing for the senses: warm water, a cup of tea, a favorite scent (from their soothing list in §11).
   - A calming place to picture, or calming sounds or textures for those who aren't visual.
   - A short walk.
   - **Distraction is allowed.** If they're too flooded for any of this, a favorite show or something funny until the wave passes is a legitimate first step. Processing can wait.
4. **Hold the send button.** If they want to reply to someone, ask them to wait until they're calm. Offer to draft a reply they can review later, and never send anything.

Check in: "On a scale of 1–10, where are you now?" Move on only when it's lower, or when they say they're ready.

## Phase 2 — Catch the script

Help them see the story their brain is telling, and give it a name.

1. **Label it:** "Sounds like the rejection script is running. Let's look at it." Labeling creates distance. Feelings are real, but they aren't facts.
   - **Let the feeling be there.** Before changing anything, allow it: "It makes sense to feel this." Don't push acceptance if naming feelings is hard for them.
   - **Step back:** invite an observer's view: "Imagine watching this happen to someone else. What do you see?" Seeing it from outside usually lowers the heat.
2. **Facts vs. story:** two columns, in their words.
   - Fact: "They haven't replied in two days."
   - Story: "They think I'm a fraud and regret talking to me."
3. **Other explanations:** "What are three other reasons this could have happened?" (They're busy, the role changed, an internal candidate, the email went to spam.)
4. **Kindness with every reframe:** "Lots of people would feel this. What would you say to a friend who got this news?" Use the user's weakest self-compassion element from §10 as the focus. Use phrases they can actually believe, small and true ("This is a hard moment." "One no doesn't decide my worth."), not big affirmations that won't land. Keep their favorites in §11.
5. **Evidence:** replay one or two real items: a win from §16, a line from the best-self portrait (§12), or a strength from §7, in other people's words where possible. Choose the ones that most directly contradict the script.

If they're replaying a conversation over and over, offer to time-box it: "Let's give it ten minutes, write down everything that's looping, then park it until tomorrow's standup." When writing it out, steer toward the wider view rather than picking apart why it happened: "How will you see this a year or two from now?" (Drawing or doodling works too.)

**Reassurance, carefully.** Asking others for reassurance can feel like a double-edged sword: embarrassing to ask, anxious not to. Don't become an endless source of reassurance either. Offer evidence, not promises ("I can't know what they think, but here's what we do know…"). If the uncertainty is the real problem, help draft one clear, low-key check-in message for them to send later, instead of waiting and spiraling.

## Phase 3 — Back to action

1. **One small step**, chosen to rebuild a sense of capability, not to fix everything: a five-minute task from their list, a walk, or reaching out to someone on their support team.
2. **Job-search mode** (after a job rejection):
   - Separate signal from noise: what, if anything, is worth learning? What should they keep doing?
   - Name the reality without self-blame: hiring has many factors they can't see or control, and it takes many tries.
   - Pick the next application, message or networking step and put it on tomorrow's plan.
3. **Reply later, if needed:** once calm, help them draft a response that is honest, brief and professional, for them to review and send themselves.
4. **Log it, with consent:** note the win of recovering ("you got back to action within the hour") in the wins log. Recovery speed is the thing to celebrate.

## Prevention (offer later, not in the moment)

- When `plan-my-day` or `meeting-prep` spots a likely rejection moment (applications, interviews, pitches, feedback), plan the reset in advance.
- **Name the dread before it wins.** When a user avoids, delays or quietly lowers their standard on something that could be judged, ask kindly whether the fear of a "no" is part of it. Then make the task smaller rather than skipping it (`make-it-smaller`, the "scary" row).
- Help them add their go-to moves, a list of soothing things, and a few believable phrases to the in-case-of-fire section (§11), so they're ready before they're needed.
- **Set expectations honestly.** Rejection sensitivity tends to be a lasting part of how someone is wired. The aim is to shrink its power and shorten recovery, not to make it disappear. Peers and support groups who get it can help too.
- **Gradual exposure, only if they choose it:** small, low-stakes "asks" that might get a no (a question to a stranger, a request for a small favor, a cold message), with a quick debrief each time. The aim is to teach the nervous system that a "no" is survivable.
- For further reading, point them to the [Cleveland Clinic's overview of RSD](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/24099-rejection-sensitive-dysphoria-rsd), a [2026 PLOS ONE study of people's lived experience of rejection sensitivity in ADHD](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0314669), ADDitude magazine's free RSD library, or [Neurodivergent Insights' practical guide to dealing with RSD](https://neurodivergentinsights.com/how-to-deal-with-rejection-sensitive-dysphoria/).
